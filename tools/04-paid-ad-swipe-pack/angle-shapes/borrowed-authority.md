# Shape 8 — Borrowed Authority

*"They do this better somewhere else. Here's what they use."*

---

## The four beats

**Headline** — names a place, a tradition or a rival market as the reason.
*Why {your audience} are choosing this over {the expensive option}.* The
authority is not the brand. It is somewhere else.

**First line of body** — states, flatly, that this place is better at this
thing. No hedging and no evidence offered, because it is presented as common
knowledge.

**Proof** — what that place does differently, in one concrete sentence. They
use formulas instead of needles. They age the ingredient for twenty months.
They source from small producers. The contrast with the local way of doing it
is the proof.

**Close** — access. You can now have the thing they have. Sometimes with an
explicit "now in {your country}".

---

## The template

> **Headline:** Why {YOUR AUDIENCE} are choosing this over {THE EXPENSIVE OR
> UNPLEASANT ALTERNATIVE}
>
> **Body:**
> In {PLACE} — {THE ONE-LINE REASON THAT PLACE HAS STANDING: the world's most
> advanced {CATEGORY} market / where they've done this for {NUMBER} years} —
> {WHAT PEOPLE THERE DO INSTEAD}.
>
> {THE CONCRETE DIFFERENCE: what they use, how long they take over it, what
> they refuse to do}. Not {THE LOCAL SHORTCUT}. Not {THE EXPENSIVE LOCAL
> OPTION}.
>
> {WHAT THAT PRODUCES, DESCRIBED PHYSICALLY} — {HOW FAST}.
>
> No {UNPLEASANT THING 1}. No {EXPENSIVE THING 2}. Just {THE RESULT}.
>
> **Close:** {PRODUCT}: the best-selling {CATEGORY} in {PLACE}, now in
> {YOUR MARKET}.

---

## Why it works

It hands the reader permission to believe a claim without you having to prove
it.

Every market has a place it already respects for a specific thing. The reader
has that belief installed before your ad arrives, so you are not building
credibility from nothing — you are borrowing credibility that already exists
and pointing it at your product. That is enormously cheaper than earning it.

It also solves a problem every new brand has: nobody has heard of you. "The
number one brand in {place}" answers that in six words. You are not unknown.
You are just new *here*.

The rejection lines are doing more than they look. Naming what you are *not* —
no needles, no three-hundred-pound creams, no clinic — recruits everyone who
already refused those options. They have made a decision about the expensive
route and this ad tells them they were right, which is a very comfortable thing
to be told.

Do not stretch this one. The place has to be genuinely and obviously connected
to the category, and the connection has to be real. If it is a claim about
where your product is made or sold, it must be true; if it is a claim about a
tradition, do not dress it up as an official endorsement.

---

## Where it shows up

**78 ads as a pitch in its own right, across 2 of the 4 brands — but present as
an ingredient in more than 400.**

- The beauty brand: **70 ads**. 60 of them on one pitch built entirely on a
  country's reputation for advanced beauty, positioned directly against
  injectables. Another 10 on a softer version of the same.
- The Italian men's brand: **8 ads** where the country *is* the pitch — one of
  them the observation that Americans spend a fortune trying to age the way
  Italians do.

The reason the standalone number understates it: this brand's biggest pitch of
all, 323 ads (Shape 7), ends every single time on a status line naming its
home country and announcing its arrival in the new market. The supplement
brand ticks "shipped from the USA" inside its spec lists. So as a *closing
line*, borrowed authority appears in well over 400 of the 2,626 ads.

Which is the real lesson here. This works as a whole ad, but it works even
harder as one line at the end of a different one.

*(Ads are counted once, under their main shape. Most real ads use more than
one.)*

## Worked example — Kirren (made-up scalp-treatment brand)

> **Why men with thinning hair are skipping the transplant clinic**
>
> In Finland, where people have spent two thousand years sitting in heat and
> then treating their skin for it, nobody has ever thought of the scalp as
> separate from the rest of the body.
>
> They treat it like skin. Birch tar, cold water, oil worked in with the
> fingers, twice a week, for a lifetime. Not a foam you spray on and hope
> about. Not a twelve-thousand-pound surgery you book at forty-five and regret
> at forty-six.
>
> The result is a scalp that isn't tight, isn't flaking, and isn't quietly
> strangling the follicles sitting in it — which you notice within about a
> month, mostly because you stop finding hair on the pillow.
>
> No surgery. No daily foam. No transplant loan.
>
> Kirren: the best-selling scalp treatment in Finland, now in the UK.

## Making the video

**Image prompt**

> A wide shot of a man in his late forties standing outside a small dark wooden
> cabin at the edge of a pine forest, early morning, cold blue light, mist
> sitting low between the trees. He is towelling his hair, steam coming off his
> shoulders. Simple, weathered, quiet. Nothing branded in shot. Documentary
> feel, not tourism advertising. Shot on a 35mm lens, natural light, slightly
> desaturated cool colour. Vertical 9:16, subject small in the frame with the
> landscape around him and space at the top for text.

**Motion note**

Open wide and stay wide for the first three seconds. The place is the argument,
so let the viewer see the place before they see the product. This is the one
shape where a landscape opener earns its keep.

Then move inward across the ad — wide, medium, close on hands working oil into
a scalp, close on a face. That inward march is the argument's shape made
visible: from *there* to *you*.

Sound matters more here than in any other shape. Wind, water, wood — no music
for the first half. Bring music in only at the "now in the UK" line, which is
where the ad stops being a documentary and becomes an offer.

**Do not fake a specific place badly.** Generated architecture, signage and
text get caught immediately by anyone who has been there. Keep it to
landscape, weather and materials, and keep any actual location claim to
something you can stand behind.
