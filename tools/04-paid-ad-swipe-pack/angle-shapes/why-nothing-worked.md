# Shape 2 — Why Nothing You Tried Worked

*"It's not that you used them wrong. They physically can't do it."*

---

## The four beats

**Headline** — a flat statement that the whole category fails, or a direct
contrast: the usual thing can't, this thing can.

**First line of body** — takes the blame off the reader immediately. *Most of
these don't work. And no, it isn't because you used them wrong.*

**Proof** — a physical, mechanical reason. Something is too big to get in.
Something never gets past the surface. Something is destroyed before it
arrives. It has to be a reason you can picture, not an opinion.

**Close** — "which is why people are switching to..." The product is presented
as the only option left standing after the explanation, not as the best of a
good bunch.

---

## The template

> **Headline:** {THE USUAL CATEGORY} can't {DO THE JOB}. {PRODUCT TYPE} can.
>
> **Body:**
> Most {CATEGORY OF PRODUCT} don't work. And no, it's not because you're using
> them wrong.
>
> It's because {PHYSICAL REASON — something is too large, too weak, in the
> wrong place, or broken down before it arrives}. They {WHAT THEY ACTUALLY DO
> INSTEAD — sit on top, wash off, get destroyed} until {THE MOMENT IT'S
> WASTED}.
>
> {THE OTHER OBVIOUS OPTION}? {WHY THAT FAILS TOO — a second, different
> mechanical reason.}
>
> And the {TREATMENTS / METHODS} that do work run {COST OR EFFORT BARRIER}.
>
> That's why {NUMBER OR GROUP} are switching to {PRODUCT} — {THE ONE
> STRUCTURAL DIFFERENCE, IN PLAIN TERMS}.
>
> **Close:** See how it actually gets in 👇

## Why it works

Everyone in a market with a stubborn problem has a graveyard of things they
bought and quit. That graveyard is the biggest obstacle you face — it is the
reason they will not believe your promise.

This shape does not argue with the graveyard. It explains it, and hands the
reader a reason that is not their fault.

The mechanical detail is what makes it land. "Ours is better" is noise.
"Collagen molecules are too big to get through skin, so they sit on top until
you wash them off" is a fact the reader can picture and repeat to a friend
that evening. Once someone can picture *why* the old thing failed, they have
built the case for a new thing themselves — and a conclusion someone reaches
on their own is far harder to argue them out of.

Knocking out the second-most-obvious alternative matters too. Without it, the
reader thinks "fine, I'll just do the other thing instead". Close that door in
one sentence.

## Where it shows up

**87 ads, across 3 of the 4 brands.**

- The beauty brand: **71 ads** on a single pitch explaining that a well-known
  ingredient is physically too large to absorb.
- The acne brand: **12 ads** on "99% of these fail, and here is the reason" —
  a story about how deep a treatment actually reaches.
- The supplement brand: **4 ads** on "most options are either too harsh or
  too weak, here's the middle".

Three brands, three products with nothing in common, one identical skeleton.

Note the size. This shape carries fewer ads than the big ones, but it does a
job none of the others do — it is the pitch that clears the wreckage before
anything else can be sold. The acne brand also runs a close cousin of it (46
more ads) that leans on a person with credentials instead of a mechanism; that
one is Shape 6.

*(Ads are counted once, under their main shape. Most real ads use more than
one.)*

## Worked example — Vantry (made-up knee-pain brand)

> **Rubs can't reach a knee joint. This can.**
>
> Most joint creams don't work. And no, it's not because you're rubbing them
> in wrong.
>
> It's because the active ingredient in a standard rub is a large molecule
> sitting in a thick base. It gets as far as the top layer of skin and stops.
> It warms the surface, you feel something happening for twenty minutes, and
> then it wears off — because it never got anywhere near the joint.
>
> Tablets? They go through your stomach and your liver first, and what's left
> is spread across your entire body. Your knee gets a fraction of a fraction.
>
> And the injections that do reach the joint run £200 a session and wear off
> in three months.
>
> That's why 40,000 people over 50 have switched to Vantry — a carrier gel
> small enough to pass through skin and deliver directly under it, not on top
> of it.
>
> See how it actually gets in 👇

## Making the video

**Image prompt**

> Extreme close-up of a man's hands, roughly age 60, rubbing white cream into
> the skin just above his knee. He is sitting on the edge of a bed in a plain
> bedroom, morning light from a window on the right. Weathered hands, visible
> knuckles, real skin texture with pores and hair. Shallow depth of field so
> the background falls away. Shot on an 85mm lens, natural light, honest
> colour, no gloss or beauty retouching. Vertical 9:16 framing, hands in the
> lower two-thirds, clear space above for text.

**Motion note**

Open tight and static on the hands for the first two seconds — the viewer
should not know what they are watching yet. Then pull back slowly to reveal
the whole person as the "and no, it's not because you're rubbing them in
wrong" line arrives.

For the mechanical explanation, switch to a simple diagram sequence rather
than more footage. Two or three cross-section frames of skin layers, one
labelled arrow stopping at the top layer and one passing through. Keep it
crude on purpose — an over-designed graphic reads as an advert, a rough one
reads as an explanation. Hold each frame two seconds with a hard cut between.

Come back to a real face for the close. The explanation earns the trust; the
face collects it.
