# Shape 6 — The Insider

*"I work in this industry. Here's what we don't say out loud."*

---

## The four beats

**Headline** — either the credential itself, or the suppression. *From a makeup
artist of twenty years.* *The industry doesn't want you knowing this.* Both do
the same job: this is coming from inside.

**First line of body** — establishes the vantage point with specifics, then
promises the forbidden part. Not "I'm an expert" but "I've done this exact
work in these exact conditions for this long — and here's what my colleagues
won't say."

**Proof** — the thing only someone inside would know. Usually it is not that
the popular option fails, but that it works *by a mechanism nobody explains* —
and that mechanism is the catch.

**Close** — what they personally use or recommend instead, offered as a
professional's choice rather than a sale.

---

## The template

> **Headline:** {THE UNCOMFORTABLE TRUTH} — from a {CREDENTIAL} of {NUMBER}
> years
>
> **Body:**
> I'm a {JOB}. I've {SPECIFIC WORK 1}, {SPECIFIC WORK 2}, and {THE HARDEST
> VERSION OF THE WORK}. And I'll tell you something most of my colleagues
> won't say out loud:
>
> {THE UNCOMFORTABLE CLAIM ABOUT THE CATEGORY, WITH A NUMBER — nine out of
> ten, most of, almost every}.
>
> I can spot it from across a room — {TELL 1}, {TELL 2}, {TELL 3}.
>
> And here's the part nobody talks about. {THE POPULAR OPTION} does work. But
> *how* it works is the problem: {THE MECHANISM, PLAINLY}. So while you're
> using it, {THE APPARENT RESULT}. The moment you stop, {WHAT COMES BACK}.
> Because {THE UNDERLYING THING} was never dealt with.
>
> {HOW THEY FOUND THE ALTERNATIVE — a colleague, a supplier, a client}.
>
> **Close:** It's what I use on {WHO THEY USE IT ON} now.

---

## Why it works

There are two claims stacked here, and the second one is the powerful one.

The first is authority: this person knows more than you. That is worth
something, but it is common and people are used to discounting it.

The second is *betrayal* — this person knows more than you **and is breaking
ranks to tell you**. That is rare, and it is irresistible. It reframes the ad
as information the reader was not supposed to receive, which is the single
most reliable way to make someone read to the end.

The "tells" list is doing sneaky work. When a professional says they can spot
something from across a room and then lists three signs, the reader
immediately checks themselves against all three. That check is the moment they
stop reading about someone else's problem.

The mechanism turn is the most sophisticated move in this pack. Notice it does
*not* say the popular option fails — arguing against something that visibly
works makes you look like a liar. It concedes the result and attacks the
method. That is much harder to dismiss, because the reader's own experience
agrees with the first half.

**One caution.** This shape puts a claimed credential in your ad. Make it a
credential you actually have, or one you can point at a real person for.
Inventing an expert is a different thing entirely and is not what this pack is
for.

---

## Where it shows up

**64 ads, across 2 of the 4 brands.**

- The acne brand: **46 ads** on a suppressed-truth pitch — a well-known
  prescription treatment does work, and here is the mechanism nobody explains,
  and here is what it means for you afterwards. This is the more advanced
  version of the shape and it is the brand's fourth-biggest pitch.
- The beauty brand: **18 ads** from a twenty-year makeup artist saying nine out
  of ten products handed to her at the counter make her clients look older,
  and that she can identify it from across a room.

It also appears as an ingredient rather than a whole pitch elsewhere — the
Italian men's brand leans on "dermatologists developed this" inside its
80-ad neck pitch (Shape 3) without building a full insider ad around it.

Worth noticing: this shape carries relatively few ads but sits inside two
brands' top five pitches. It is not a volume play. It is the pitch that
converts the sceptical.

*(Ads are counted once, under their main shape. Most real ads use more than
one.)*

## Worked example — Merrow (made-up teeth-whitening brand)

> **Why your teeth go grey a year after whitening — from a hygienist of 18
> years**
>
> I'm a dental hygienist. Eighteen years, three practices, and about forty
> thousand mouths. Here's something most of my colleagues won't say out loud:
> most of the whitening kits my patients buy are making their teeth worse in
> the long run.
>
> I can spot a strip user across the room — the grey shadow at the gum line,
> the two front teeth brighter than the rest, and the wincing when they drink
> anything cold.
>
> And here's the part nobody talks about. The strips do work. But *how* they
> work is the problem: high-concentration peroxide sitting on enamel for half
> an hour opens up the surface and lifts stain out of it. While you're using
> them, they're genuinely white. Three months later they're greyer than when
> you started — because opened-up enamel takes on stain faster than it did
> before. So you buy another box.
>
> A supplier showed me what practices actually use between appointments — a
> low-concentration gel with hydroxyapatite that rebuilds the surface while it
> lifts. Slower. Two weeks instead of two days.
>
> It's what I use on my own teeth, and it's what I sell at reception now.

## Making the video

**Image prompt**

> A woman in her forties in dark blue clinical scrubs, standing in a plain
> treatment room, arms loosely folded, talking directly to camera. Hair tied
> back, no jewellery, a lanyard at her chest. Bright, even, slightly cool
> overhead lighting — clinical, not flattering. Simple background: a sink, a
> cabinet, one piece of equipment out of focus behind her. Confident, matter
> of fact, entirely unglamorous. Shot on a 35mm lens, locked off, honest
> colour. Vertical 9:16, subject centred with headroom for text.

**Motion note**

This is the one shape that should be locked off on a tripod. Handheld reads as
a friend confiding; a still, level frame reads as someone who does this
professionally and is not nervous about being recorded. Steady is the whole
character.

Straight down the lens for the entire ad, with one exception: a small glance
away before the "most of my colleagues won't say" line. That half-second of
hesitation before breaking ranks is the most valuable frame in the ad.

Cut to a demonstration for the mechanism — hands, a model of teeth, a close-up,
anything physical. Come back to her face for the last line. Do not put a price,
a discount or a countdown anywhere in this one. The credibility is the offer,
and a discount graphic destroys it instantly.
