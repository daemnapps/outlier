# Shape 4 — The Spec List

*The biggest shape in the entire sweep. Not a story. A stack of ticks.*

---

## The four beats

**Headline** — plain and functional. Names the product or the one thing that
makes it different. No cleverness, no mystery, no hook. It is a label.

**First line of body** — one question aimed straight at the outcome the reader
wants, phrased as "do you want to {the result}, naturally / finally / without
{the usual cost}?" One line, then straight into the list.

**Proof** — the list. Six to eight ticked lines, every one a different kind of
proof so no two feel like the same claim twice: what it does, how much of it
there is, who tested it, how long it took to make, where it ships from, how
many people bought it.

**Close** — a direct instruction. Buy, shop, see how it works. No softening.

---

## The template

> **Headline:** {THE ONE-LINE DESCRIPTION OF WHAT MAKES IT DIFFERENT}
>
> **Body:**
> {THE OUTCOME THEY WANT, AS A QUESTION}?
>
> {PRODUCT} — {ONE PLAIN SENTENCE SAYING WHAT IT IS AND WHAT IT'S MADE OF}.
>
> ✅ {WHAT IT DOES — outcome 1}
> ✅ {WHAT IT DOES — outcome 2, different area of life}
> ✅ {THE AMOUNT — an actual number and unit}
> ✅ {THE PROCESS — how it's made, how long it takes, what's unusual about it}
> ✅ {THE INDEPENDENT CHECK — tested by, approved by, studied in}
> ✅ {THE ABSENCE — no filler, no sugar, no {THING THEY'RE AVOIDING}}
> ✅ {THE ORIGIN — made in, shipped from}
> ✅ {THE SCALE — number of orders, number of reviews, rating}
>
> **Close:** {DIRECT INSTRUCTION} 👇

---

## Why it works

It looks like information rather than persuasion, and people let information
in.

There is no story to disbelieve. There is no character to distrust. There is
just a list, and a list reads as a specification sheet — the kind of thing you
scan before buying a kettle. The reader's guard never comes up because nothing
in it feels like it is trying.

The variety in the list is what does the actual convincing. Eight lines of "it
works" would read as one claim repeated eight times. But a dose, then a
process, then a test, then a country, then a review count — that is five
different *kinds* of proof, and each one covers a different reason someone
might say no. Someone worried it is underdosed is answered by the number.
Someone worried it is a scam is answered by the testing. Someone worried it is
shipping from nowhere is answered by the origin.

It is also the cheapest shape in the pack to make forty versions of. Change
the opening question, reorder the ticks, swap which claim goes first — and you
have a genuinely different ad without writing a new one. Which is exactly what
these brands do.

## Where it shows up

**949 ads, across 3 of the 4 brands. The largest shape in the sweep by a
long way — 36% of every ad counted.**

- The supplement brand: **488 ads**, which is 98% of everything it runs. This
  brand is essentially one shape. Its single biggest pitch, 313 ads, is a spec
  list with seven ticked lines.
- The beauty brand: **404 ads**, including its biggest pitch at 332 — a
  three-tick version, much shorter than the supplement brand's, ending on
  "see how it works".
- The acne brand: **57 ads**, a version aimed at parents that opens by naming
  the two things they care about and then ticks them off.

Three brands, three completely unrelated products, and it is the number-one
shape for two of them.

*(Ads are counted once, under their main shape. Most real ads use more than
one.)*

## Making the video

**Image prompt**

> A single dark amber supplement bottle standing on a pale oak bedside table,
> beside a folded linen throw and a small glass of water. Warm low lamp light
> from the right, deep soft shadows, the rest of the bedroom dark and out of
> focus. The label is sharp and fully legible, facing camera straight on.
> Calm, still, expensive-feeling, nothing else in shot. Shot on a 50mm lens,
> shallow depth of field. Vertical 9:16, product in the lower half, generous
> dark space above it for text.

**Motion note**

This shape barely needs motion, and that is a feature — it is the cheapest
video in the pack to make, which is why brands build hundreds of them.

Hold on the product with a very slow rotation, about 15 degrees over six
seconds, so the light moves across the label. That single shot can carry the
whole ad.

The list is the edit. Bring each ticked line on screen one at a time, about
0.8 seconds apart, stacking rather than replacing — so the ticks accumulate
and the viewer physically watches the proof pile up. Do not animate them in
with anything fancy; a plain fade is right. The rhythm of the stack is doing
the work.

Two things worth adding if you want it to feel less like a slide: a hand
entering frame to pick up the bottle on the final tick, and a subtle real-world
sound — capsules moving in the bottle, water being set down.

**Use your real product photo where you can.** This is the one shape where a
generated bottle will get caught, because the viewer is reading the label.
