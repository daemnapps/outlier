# Shape 10 — The Price Close

*The smallest shape in the sweep. It is in here because of how small it is.*

---

## The four beats

**Headline** — the number and the safety net together. *50% off + 30-day
guarantee.* *Secret sale, up to 60% off, ends tonight.*

**First line of body** — not the price. Either the scale of the business
(orders shipped, rating, customer count) or a callback to money the reader has
already wasted on the wrong thing.

**Proof** — the guarantee, stated so plainly it removes the decision. Full
refund. No questions. The risk moves from the buyer to the seller in one line.

**Close** — the deadline, and only then the instruction.

---

## The template

> **Headline:** {DISCOUNT} + {GUARANTEE LENGTH} guarantee
>
> **Body:**
> {SCALE PROOF: 📦 over {NUMBER} orders shipped. ⭐ rated {SCORE} by {NUMBER}
> customers.}
>
> — or, if you don't have scale yet —
>
> {THE SUNK COST: You've already spent more on {WRONG PURCHASES} than this
> would cost you. {ITEMISE TWO OR THREE OF THEM}.}
>
> {THE PRODUCT IN ONE LINE — what it is, the number that matters.}
> ✅ {NO BAD THING 1} ✅ {NO BAD THING 2} ✅ {THE CHECK — third-party tested}
>
> 💰 Right now it's {PRICE} instead of {FULL PRICE}, and every order is backed
> by a {LENGTH} money-back guarantee.
> 🛡️ Not happy? Full refund, no questions asked.
>
> **Close:** ⏰ {THE DEADLINE}. {INSTRUCTION}.

---

## Why it works — and why it barely gets used

The guarantee is the working part, not the discount.

A discount answers "is this worth the money", which is rarely the actual
objection. The guarantee answers "what if I'm wrong about this", which almost
always is. Sixty days and a full refund makes the decision reversible, and
people will make a reversible decision far faster than a cheap one.

The sunk-cost opener is the sharpest thing in this shape. *You've already spent
more on the wrong ones than this costs.* It reframes the purchase as recovering
from a mistake rather than taking a risk, and it lands hardest on exactly the
person who has tried three things already — who is also the person most likely
to hesitate.

**But look at the numbers before you build your account around this.**

Across all 2,626 ads, pitches whose *headline leads with a discount* account
for **11 ads. That is 0.4%.** Two brands run one each — four ads and seven ads.

That is the finding. These brands are spending almost nothing on shouting about
price. They spend it on explaining a problem, contradicting a belief, or listing
what is in the box. Price shows up at the *end* of those ads, as the last line
of a longer argument — the acne brand's biggest scarcity pitch (54 ads) carries
a discount, a free gift and a guarantee, but only after the stock warning has
done the work.

The lesson runs directly against most beginners' instinct, which is to lead
with the offer because the offer feels like the strongest thing they have. The
people spending the most money here have concluded the opposite: **earn the
attention with an argument, then close with the price.** A discount opens
nothing. It only closes.

---

## Where it shows up

**11 ads as a standalone pitch, across 2 of the 4 brands. The smallest shape
in the pack by a wide margin.**

- The beauty brand: **7 ads** on a sale headline that opens with the money the
  reader has already wasted on wrong-shade products before it mentions its own
  price.
- The supplement brand: **4 ads** on a discount headline that opens with a
  million orders shipped and a 4.9 rating, ticks three quality claims, and only
  then names the price.

As a *closing block* inside other shapes it is much more common — roughly 90
further ads across three brands end on a discount, a guarantee or both without
ever leading with one.

Neither brand that runs it leads with the price. Both establish something else
first, in the first line, before the number appears.

*(Ads are counted once, under their main shape. Most real ads use more than
one.)*

## Worked example — Tallow & Fern (made-up body butter brand)

> **40% off + a 90-day guarantee 🔥**
>
> You've already spent more on lotions that didn't work than this jar costs.
> The one that stung. The one that smelled like a hospital. The £34 one you
> used twice and moved to the back of the cupboard.
>
> Tallow & Fern is six ingredients, whipped, in a 200ml jar. Enough for about
> eleven weeks.
>
> ✅ No fragrance ✅ No fillers ✅ Made in small batches in Yorkshire
>
> 💰 Right now it's £17 instead of £28, and every jar is backed by a 90-day
> money-back guarantee.
> 🛡️ Doesn't fix the dry patches on your shins? Full refund. Keep the jar.
>
> ⏰ Sale ends Sunday night. Grab one 👇

## Making the video

**Image prompt**

> An open jar of pale whipped body butter on a worn wooden kitchen table, lid
> resting beside it, next to two half-used bottles of other lotion pushed
> slightly out of focus in the background. Warm late-afternoon side light from
> a window on the left. Real, domestic, a bit untidy — a table someone
> actually uses. The jar's label is sharp and legible. Shot on a 50mm lens,
> shallow depth of field. Vertical 9:16, jar in the lower third, warm space
> above for text.

**Motion note**

Keep it short. Fifteen seconds at most. This shape is aimed at someone who has
already seen your other ads — it is a nudge, not an argument, and a long one
wastes the moment.

Give the sunk-cost opener a visual: a hand pushing the old bottles aside and
setting the jar down in their place. One movement, and the whole first line is
made without saying it.

Put the guarantee on screen as text and leave it there for the last four
seconds — longer than any other element. It is the part that closes and it
should be the last thing on screen.

The deadline can be a plain text line. Do not use a ticking countdown graphic
unless the deadline is real and the ad actually stops. And do not build most of
your ads out of this shape. On this evidence, one in every two hundred is about
right.
