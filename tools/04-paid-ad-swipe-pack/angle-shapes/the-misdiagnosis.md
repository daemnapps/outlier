# Shape 1 — The Misdiagnosis

*"You don't have one problem. You have three, and they share a cause."*

---

## The four beats

**Headline** — names the reader's problem, then quietly signals they have been
reading it wrong. Not "buy this" — "understand this".

**First line of body** — plays the reader's own week back at them. Specific,
dated, sequenced. Monday this, Tuesday that, Wednesday the other. The reader
recognises themselves in the list before any claim has been made.

**Proof** — the reframe. Those separate symptoms are not separate. They share
one hidden cause that nobody told them about, and the product is aimed at the
cause rather than the symptoms.

**Close** — soft. Learn how this works. Find out which one you have. It asks
for attention, not money.

---

## The template

> **Headline:** Understand your {PROBLEM} type
>
> **Body:**
> {DAY 1}: {SPECIFIC SYMPTOM, WITH ITS LOCATION}.
> {DAY 2}: {A DIFFERENT SPECIFIC SYMPTOM, DIFFERENT LOCATION}.
> {DAY 3}: {A THIRD, DIFFERENT AGAIN}.
>
> Your {BODY PART / SITUATION} isn't broken. It's dealing with more than one
> thing at the same time.
>
> Most people don't have "one {PROBLEM}". They have a combination — and each
> one has a different trigger:
> — {TRIGGER 1}
> — {TRIGGER 2}
> — {TRIGGER 3}
>
> Here's the part almost nobody tells you: every one of those goes back to the
> same {ROOT CAUSE}. Which is why {WHAT THEY'VE BEEN DOING} was never going to
> fix it — it only ever reached {THE SURFACE / THE SYMPTOM}.
>
> {PRODUCT} works on {ROOT CAUSE} instead.
>
> **Close:** Find out which type you're dealing with 👇

---

## Why it works

Because it does not argue with the reader — it explains them.

Someone with a long-running problem has already been told to try harder and
already blamed themselves for it not working. This shape takes that away in
one move: *it wasn't you, and it wasn't one thing.* That is a relief, and
relief buys attention.

The day-by-day opening is doing quiet work too. It is not describing a generic
customer, it is describing *this* week — which is what makes the reader stop.
And once they accept "I have three things, not one", they have also accepted
that anything aimed at only one of them was always going to fail. The pitch
for the product is already made before the product is mentioned.

It also completely sidesteps the defence people have against ads. Nothing is
being sold in the first three lines. Something is being explained.

## Where it shows up

**253 ads, across 2 of the 4 brands.**

The acne brand runs **247 of those** — it is the single largest thing that
brand does. One headline in this shape carries **202 ads on its own**, run
three separate times with the body rewritten underneath it each time.

The beauty brand runs a smaller version of the same shape (6 ads), asking why
a product turns the wrong colour after a certain age and answering with a
mechanism rather than a promise.

Same structure, two unrelated markets — one selling to teenagers, one selling
to women over 50.

*(Ads are counted once, under their main shape. Most real ads use more than
one.)*

> **Understand your bloating type**
>
> Monday: flat in the morning, three inches bigger by 6pm.
> Wednesday: fine all day, wrecked an hour after dinner.
> Saturday: bloated from the moment you wake up, and you ate nothing unusual.
>
> Your gut isn't broken. It's dealing with more than one thing at the same
> time.
>
> Most people don't have "a bloating problem". They have a combination — and
> each one has a different trigger:
> — food moving through too slowly
> — gas made by the wrong bacteria
> — a gut lining that's inflamed and reacting to everything
>
> Here's the part almost nobody tells you: all three trace back to the same
> thing — the balance of what's living in your gut. Which is why cutting out
> foods one at a time never worked. You were removing triggers instead of
> fixing what makes you sensitive to them.
>
> Kettlebrook works on the balance instead.
>

## Making the video

**Image prompt**

> A woman in her mid-thirties standing side-on in a plain bathroom, morning
> light from a small window on the left, wearing a simple grey vest and soft
> cotton shorts. She is looking down at her stomach with a flat, tired
> expression — not distressed, just familiar with it. Hand resting on her
> waist. Shot on a 50mm lens, natural light only, slightly muted colour, real
> skin texture, no makeup, no retouching. Vertical 9:16 framing, subject
> slightly right of centre with empty wall space above her head for text.

**Motion note**

Almost no camera movement — a very slow push in, about 5% over four seconds.
The realism is the point; a moving camera makes it feel produced. Have her
exhale and let her shoulders drop as the push begins.

For the day-by-day lines, cut to three near-identical shots in the same
bathroom, same framing, different clothes each time — so the days read as
days. Hold each for about a second and a half. Put the day name in plain text
at the top of the frame.

Then break the pattern hard for the reframe line: cut to a completely
different setup — close on her face, softer light, camera now still — and let
the line "your gut isn't broken" land in silence before the voice comes back.
The visual change is what tells the viewer the argument just turned.
