# Shape 9 — Running Out

*Two versions of the same shape, and one of them is much better than the
other.*

---

## The four beats

**Headline** — the stock situation, stated as news. A low-stock warning, an
exact count of units left, or a flat admission that you have sold out.

**First line of body** — the position the reader is in right now. You still
have time, but not much. Or: you have been thinking about this, and now
everyone else is buying it.

**Proof** — and this is where the two versions split — **a reason for the
shortage**. Version A: demand is beating production. Version B: we deliberately
make it in a way that runs out.

**Close** — Version A pushes to buy now. Version B asks you to wait, which
sounds insane and is the more effective of the two.

---

## The template — Version A, demand

> **Headline:** {STOCK STATUS, STATED AS NEWS}
>
> **Body:**
> If you're seeing this, you still have time — but not much.
>
> {THE OFFER} is live now, and we're already running low. Why? Because this
> {SEASON / EVENT} we're doing:
> — {OFFER ELEMENT 1}
> — {OFFER ELEMENT 2}
> — {THE GUARANTEE}
>
> {THE PRODUCTION REALITY: production is {NUMBER} weeks behind demand / we can't
> make them fast enough}.
>
> If you miss this, you're looking at {THE SPECIFIC CONSEQUENCE — a waitlist of
> {NUMBER} weeks, the price going back to {AMOUNT}}.
>
> **Close:** {DIRECT INSTRUCTION}.

## The template — Version B, principle

> **Headline:** {PRODUCT} is out of stock 😱
>
> **Body:**
> We {THE SOURCING CHOICE — source from small producers / make it in batches of
> {NUMBER} / wait for the {HARVEST / SEASON}}. When {THE SUPPLY EVENT} ends,
> production stops.
>
> We don't {THE SHORTCUT 1}. We don't {THE SHORTCUT 2}. We wait.
>
> That's why we run out. And that's why {NUMBER} {CUSTOMERS} trust what's in
> {THE PACKAGING} — because it never changes based on what's convenient for us.
>
> **Close:** In stock? Try it for {NUMBER} days, full refund if you're not
> convinced. Sold out? I'll ask you to wait.

---

## Why it works

Version A works because it converts people who had already decided. Someone who
has read three of your ads and not bought needs a reason to act today, and a
deadline is that reason. It is genuinely useful and it genuinely works.

But it has a cost, and the cost is why Version B exists. Everyone has seen a
thousand fake countdowns. A stock warning from a brand you do not know is
evidence of nothing except that they run stock warnings, and if the reader
checks tomorrow and it is still there, you have taught them not to believe
anything else you say either.

Version B does something much cleverer. It takes the same fact — we run out —
and uses it as **proof of quality instead of a reason to hurry**. The shortage
stops being a sales tactic and becomes evidence that the brand refuses to cut
corners. Every shortcut it names and rejects is a corner your competitors are
implied to be cutting.

Then it does the thing almost no ad ever does: it tells the reader not to buy.
"Sold out? I'll ask you to wait" is a sentence with no commercial upside in the
moment, which is exactly why it reads as true. A brand willing to lose today's
sale is a brand that is not desperate — and people buy from businesses that are
not desperate.

**Only run either version if it is true.** The whole mechanism is credibility,
and a fake shortage burns credibility permanently.

---

## Where it shows up

**113 ads, across 3 of the 4 brands.**

- The acne brand: **74 ads** — a stock warning attached to a seasonal sale (54)
  and a restock announcement that turns into an explanation of the problem
  (20). Pure Version A.
- The beauty brand: **35 ads** built around an exact count of remaining units,
  with production described as three weeks behind demand and a waitlist named
  as the consequence. Version A, at its most concrete.
- The Italian men's brand: **4 ads** — the only Version B in the whole sweep.
  Small-batch sourcing, production stops when the harvest ends, and an explicit
  invitation to wait.

Note the ratio. Scarcity is **4.3% of everything running** across all four
brands. It is a supporting pitch, not a main one — none of these brands leads
with it. Use it the way they do: as one pitch among several, not as your
personality.

Also note that the specific number outperforms the vague one. A headline
naming the exact number of units left is checkable in a way "limited stock"
never is, and it is the version carrying the second-largest count here.

*(Ads are counted once, under their main shape. Most real ads use more than
one.)*

## Worked example — Braemar (made-up wool insole brand)

*Version B, because it is the one worth learning.*

> **Braemar is sold out 😱**
>
> Our wool comes off about forty hill farms in one part of Scotland, and it
> comes off them once a year. When that clip is spun, that's the year's
> production. There isn't a second source.
>
> We don't blend it out with synthetic to make it go further. We don't switch
> to imported wool for the winter and quietly say nothing. We wait for
> shearing.
>
> That's why we sell out every February, and it's why 31,000 people buy the
> same pair again the following year — because what's in the packet in year
> four is what was in it in year one.
>
> In stock? Try them for 60 days and send them back if they're not the warmest
> thing you own. Sold out? I'm going to ask you to wait until May.

## Making the video

**Image prompt**

> A pair of weathered hands holding a loose handful of raw undyed wool in a
> cold stone farm building, low grey daylight coming through an open doorway
> behind. Dust in the air. Rough textures everywhere — stone, wood, wool,
> skin. No product, no packaging, no branding anywhere in the shot. Documentary
> photography, honest colour, nothing styled. Shot on a 50mm lens, shallow
> focus on the wool. Vertical 9:16, hands centred low with the dark interior
> above for text.

**Motion note**

Slow everything down. Version B's whole claim is patience, so a fast-cut,
urgent edit contradicts the words on screen. Long holds, four or five seconds
each, and a slow drift rather than any push.

Show the process, not the product. Wool, hands, a machine turning, a shelf of
empty boxes. The empty shelf is the money shot — it is the proof, and it is the
one image that says "sold out" without a graphic.

No countdown timer. No flashing text. No red. Every urgency device you add
moves this ad from Version B back to Version A and throws away the thing that
made it work.

For Version A instead, invert all of the above: hard cuts, a real counter on
screen, the actual number of units remaining, and the deadline stated out loud.
Just make sure the number is real and the ad comes down when it hits zero.
