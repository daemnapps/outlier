# Shape 3 — The Visible Tell

*"It's not the thing you've been worrying about. It's the one other people
actually notice."*

---

## The four beats

**Headline** — names one specific, visible body detail, then dismisses it in
the same breath. Two or three words, a question mark, and a refusal. *Sagging
neck? Not anymore.*

**First line of body** — the redirect. The thing the reader has been fixing is
not the thing giving them away. It is this other, smaller, very specific thing
— and everyone can see it.

**Proof** — anatomy. Name the muscle, the layer, the structure. Explain in
three plain sentences what is physically happening under there and why. This
is not decoration; specificity is the entire proof.

**Close** — the targeted fix. Built for this one area, not a general product
borrowed from somewhere else.

---

## The template

> **Headline:** {VISIBLE BODY DETAIL}? Not anymore.
>
> **Body:**
> That {DETAIL} is {THE COST — ageing you ten years / the first thing people
> notice / what everyone reads before you speak}.
>
> The #1 sign that says "{THE LABEL THEY FEAR}" isn't {THE OBVIOUS THING
> EVERYONE FIXES}. It's {THE SPECIFIC DETAIL} — and no {WORKAROUND THEY
> CURRENTLY USE} hides it.
>
> Here's what's happening under there. {NAME THE ANATOMICAL PART}, {WHAT IT
> IS, IN ONE PLAIN SENTENCE}. With {TIME / AGE / THE CAUSE}, {WHAT CHANGES}.
> The {STRUCTURE} loses its support and the {SURFACE} above it {WHAT YOU
> SEE} — {SPECIFIC VISIBLE RESULT 1}, {RESULT 2}, {RESULT 3}.
>
> Unlike {THE GENERAL PRODUCT PEOPLE USE INSTEAD}, which barely works on
> {WHY THIS AREA IS DIFFERENT}, {PRODUCT} was built for exactly this.
>
> **Close:** Here's the fix 👇

## Why it works

People do not worry about ageing in general. They worry about one thing they
saw in one photograph.

This shape finds that thing and says it out loud — which is uncomfortable, and
that is why it stops the scroll. A general promise about looking younger slides
straight past. "Your neck" does not.

The redirect is the clever part. It tells the reader that the thing they have
been diligently working on was never the problem, which does two things at
once: it explains why their effort has not paid off, and it makes the new
detail urgent, because it has apparently been on display this whole time
without them knowing.

The anatomy is what turns it from insult into information. Naming the actual
muscle and explaining what happens to it changes the tone completely — from
someone pointing at your flaw to someone showing you a diagram. And a product
"built specifically for this area" is automatically more believable than a
general one, because the reader has just been told this area is different.

Handle with care. The line between "someone is telling me something useful"
and "someone is being cruel about my body" is thin, and it sits in the second
sentence. Name the detail, then move to the mechanism fast. Do not linger.

## Where it shows up

**83 ads, effectively all in 1 brand — with a clear echo in a second.**

The Italian men's face-treatment brand runs it as its second-biggest pitch:
**80 ads** on a neckline, plus **3 more** on the same subject in Italian, using
the same skeleton — name the detail, name the muscle underneath it, explain why
a general face product cannot help there.

The beauty brand does not run it as a full pitch, but the same move appears
inside other ads: a specific visible line at the jaw described as the giveaway
that everyone else can spot.

Included here despite the single-brand concentration because 83 ads on one
pitch is more than the discount and deadline pitches across all four brands
combined — and because it is the most transferable shape in the pack. Every
market has a tell.

## Worked example — Cordell (made-up men's under-eye brand)

> **Hollow under-eyes? Not anymore.**
>
> Those shadows are the reason people keep asking if you're tired when you
> slept eight hours.
>
> The #1 thing that reads as "worn out" on a man isn't wrinkles — it's the
> hollow under the eye, and no amount of sleep or water fills it back in.
>
> Here's what's happening under there. Sitting beneath your lower eyelid is a
> small pad of fat that holds the skin forward and catches the light. From
> your late thirties it starts to shrink and drop. The skin above it has
> nothing to sit on any more, so it falls into the gap — and the light stops
> bouncing off your cheek and starts pooling under your eye. That's the
> shadow. It isn't a colour. It's a dent.
>
> Unlike face creams, which are built for skin ten times thicker than the skin
> under your eye, Cordell was made for exactly this — a firming gel that
> thickens the layer over that pad so it catches light again.
>
> Here's the fix 👇

## Making the video

**Image prompt**

> Tight close-up of a man's face, age 45 to 50, from the eyebrows to the top
> lip, turned three-quarters to camera. Hard side light from the left throwing
> a clear shadow under the eye — the shadow is the subject of the shot. Short
> dark hair going grey at the temple, light stubble, real pores and fine
> lines, no retouching whatsoever. Neutral expression, looking slightly off
> camera. Plain dark grey background. Shot on a 100mm macro lens, shallow
> focus on the eye. Vertical 9:16, face filling the frame, small space at the
> bottom for text.

**Motion note**

Open on the shadow, not on the man. The first frame should be so tight the
viewer cannot tell what they are looking at, then pull back over two seconds
to reveal the face as the headline lands.

Move the light, not the camera. Have the key light travel slowly from side-on
to front-on across the anatomy explanation — the shadow visibly filling in as
the mechanism is described. It illustrates the whole argument without a single
graphic, and it is the one bit of craft that makes this shape feel expensive.

Keep his expression completely flat throughout. Any hint of sadness turns
information into pity and the ad stops working. He is not upset about his
face; he is being shown something about it.
