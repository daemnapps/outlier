# Shape 7 — The Two-Minute Fix

*For the person who agrees they have the problem and will not do anything
complicated about it.*

---

## The four beats

**Headline** — names the problem and refuses it in the same breath, with the
brand as the reason. Two or three words, a question mark, and a refusal.

**First line of body** — concedes that the problem is real and inevitable. It
does not fight the reader's belief; it agrees with it, naming two causes
nobody can argue with. Then the pivot: but what can you actually do about it?

**Proof** — the effort, stated as a number. Two minutes a day. Twenty seconds
before bed. Under a minute. The number is the entire proof, because the
reader's real objection was never "does it work", it was "I'm not doing a
routine".

**Close** — identity, not instruction. It says something flattering about the
kind of person who does this small thing.

---

## The template

> **Headline:** {THE PROBLEM}? Not with {PRODUCT}.
>
> **Body:**
> {THE CAUSE} leaves its mark. So does {THE OTHER, UNAVOIDABLE CAUSE}. But
> what can you actually do about it?
>
> Easy. Just {SMALL NUMBER} {MINUTES / SECONDS} a day.
>
> That's all it takes to {THE OUTCOME}. {WHEN YOU DO IT — before bed, with
> your coffee, on the way out}. {WHAT YOU SEE, AND WHEN}.
>
> {PRODUCT}: {THE STATUS LINE — the best-selling {CATEGORY} in {PLACE}, now in
> {YOUR MARKET}}.
>
> **Close:** For {THE KIND OF PERSON THEY'D LIKE TO BE}. {EMOJI}

---

## Why it works

Most ads argue about whether the product works. This one skips that argument
entirely and attacks the real objection, which is almost always effort.

Think about who is being sold to here. A man in his fifties who has never used
a face product in his life is not sceptical about whether creams do anything —
he is certain he is not becoming a person with a skincare routine. Ten steps
is a no before he has read the second one. Two minutes is not a routine. Two
minutes is nothing.

The concession at the top is what buys the right to make the offer. By
agreeing that ageing and stress are unavoidable, the ad establishes that it is
not going to make a ridiculous promise — which is exactly what this reader was
braced for. A modest ad to a sceptical reader outperforms a big one every
time.

And the close does something worth stealing. It does not say "buy now", it
says *this is what people like you do*. It converts a purchase into a small
piece of self-respect, which is a far better reason to spend twenty pounds
than a discount is.

This is also the cheapest shape in the pack to produce, and it is short —
which is why one brand can run three hundred versions of it.

---

## Where it shows up

**334 ads, across 2 of the 4 brands — and it is the single most concentrated
pitch in the entire sweep.**

- The Italian men's face-treatment brand: **323 ads**. One script, run four
  separate times with tiny rewrites — 259 ads, then 44, then 17, then 3. That
  one script is **70% of everything that brand is running**. In one of the four
  versions they even swapped the product name inside the body while leaving the
  script otherwise untouched, which tells you they were testing the name, not
  the pitch.
- The acne brand: **11 ads** on a much shorter version — clearer skin in under
  one minute, and nothing else.

The beauty brand runs the same argument inside a different shape: no needles,
no appointments, results in seconds. Filed under Shape 8 because its proof is
geographic, but the effort promise is identical.

Three hundred and twenty-three ads on one script is the loudest single signal
in this data. If you take one thing from this pack, take this: **when
something works, they do not move on.**

*(Ads are counted once, under their main shape. Most real ads use more than
one.)*

## Worked example — Halden (made-up men's face balm)

> **Looking rough by Wednesday? Not with Halden.**
>
> Late nights leave a mark. So does being forty-three. But what are you
> supposed to do about it — a ten-step routine?
>
> No. Twenty seconds.
>
> One balm, straight after you shave, while your face is still damp. That's
> it. By the end of the first week the tightness is gone and you stop looking
> like you're running on four hours even when you are.
>
> Halden: the best-selling men's balm in Norway, now in the UK.
>
> For men who don't have time to look tired. 🪓

## Making the video

**Image prompt**

> A man in his mid-forties standing at a bathroom sink, seen in the mirror,
> patting a small amount of balm onto his cheek with two fingers. Short hair,
> greying at the sides, towel over one shoulder, plain dark t-shirt. Ordinary
> family bathroom — a toothbrush, a tap, tiles — not a design magazine. Cool
> morning light from a frosted window. Unhurried, slightly unimpressed
> expression, like this is the most normal thing in the world. Shot on a 35mm
> lens, natural light, real skin texture. Vertical 9:16, framed through the
> mirror with space above for text.

**Motion note**

Film it in real time and let the length prove the claim. If the ad says twenty
seconds, show something close to twenty seconds of an unremarkable man doing an
unremarkable thing. Speeding it up or cutting it into six pieces implies it
takes longer than you are admitting.

One shot is enough. A slow drift from a wide of the bathroom to a closer mirror
framing, no cuts. The absence of editing is the message: this is not an
operation.

Put a plain timer or a simple counter in the corner if you want the effort
claim to be undeniable. Cheap, and it makes the promise checkable.

Keep his expression flat and slightly bored. Any enthusiasm turns him into an
actor and the man watching stops believing this is for him.
