# What the spend says

Four brands. **2,626 live ads** running across **223 different pitches** at the
moment they were swept.

Nobody at these companies will ever tell you what is working. They do not have
to. The shape of what they are running says it out loud.

**Before anything else, one honesty note.** What was counted is *live ads*,
not dollars. Nobody outside these companies can see the actual budget. But the
number of versions a brand has built and is still running is the closest
public stand-in there is — because building fifty versions of a pitch costs
real money and real hours, and no team does that for a pitch that is losing.
Treat every number here as "how hard they are leaning", not "how much they
spent".

---

## 1. They are not running lots of ideas. They are running one.

The first thing to look at is how much of a brand sits on its top three
pitches.

| Brand | Share on top 3 pitches | Total pitches running |
|---|---|---|
| Supplement brand, heart and gut capsules | **91%** | 8 |
| Italian men's face treatment | **83%** | 35 |
| Beauty brand, women 45+ | **73%** | 18 |
| Acne brand, teens and parents | **29%** | 162 |

Read the top three rows again. Three of four brands have **73% to 91% of
everything they are running** sitting on three ideas.

Go one level tighter — a single pitch, the best one each brand has:

- Supplement brand: **313 of 496 ads** — 63% of the whole account on one pitch
- Men's face treatment: **259 of 463 ads** — 56% on one pitch
- Beauty brand: **332 of 830 ads** — 40% on one pitch
- Acne brand: **97 of 837 ads** — 12% on one pitch

One idea carrying half a company's advertising is not an accident and it is
not laziness. It is what happens when you find something that works and stop
apologising for it.

**What this means for you:** the instinct to run ten different ideas so you
"cover your bases" is the opposite of what the people spending the most money
do. They find one and they hammer it.

---

## 2. When something works, they build dozens of versions of it

Divide each brand's ads by the number of distinct pitches and you get how many
versions they build per idea:

| Brand | Ads | Pitches | Versions per pitch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Supplement brand | 496 | 8 | **62** |
| Beauty brand | 830 | 18 | **46** |
| Men's face treatment | 463 | 35 | **13** |
| Acne brand | 837 | 162 | **5** |

Sixty-two versions of one idea. Not sixty-two ideas.

And look at what the versions actually are. Across all four brands, the same
headline turns up over and over with the body copy rewritten underneath it:

- The acne brand runs one headline **three separate times** — 97 ads, then 86,
  then 19 — with the body reworded each time. That is **202 ads on one
  headline.**
- The supplement brand runs one headline **three times** — 313, 20 and 18 ads.
  **351 ads on one headline.**
- The men's face brand runs one headline **four times** — 259, 44, 17 and 3
  ads. **323 ads on one headline.** In one of those versions they even swapped
  the product name inside the body copy while keeping the script identical.
- The beauty brand runs its best headline **twice** — 332 ads, then 39.

So the real unit of work at these companies is not "come up with an ad". It is
"we have a winner — now make forty more of it". New angle of the same face.
New voice reading the same script. Same words, different pace. Same idea,
tighter.

**What this means for you:** the moment something works, your job stops being
invention and starts being multiplication. This is exactly the part a machine
should do rather than a person.

---

## 3. Almost all of it is video — except in one telling case

| Brand | Video | Still image | Video share |
|---|---|---|---|
| Men's face treatment | 443 | 20 | **96%** |
| Supplement brand | 448 | 48 | **90%** |
| Beauty brand | 614 | 216 | **74%** |
| Acne brand | 156 | 681 | **19%** |

Overall: **1,661 videos to 965 images — 63% video.**

Now put that next to the concentration table, because they line up almost
perfectly:

| Brand | Concentrated on top 3 | Video share |
|---|---|---|
| Supplement brand | 91% | 90% |
| Men's face treatment | 83% | 96% |
| Beauty brand | 73% | 74% |
| Acne brand | 29% | 19% |

The three brands that know what is working are running video. The one that is
still searching — 162 different pitches, only 29% concentrated, five versions
per idea — is running **81% still images**.

That is not a coincidence, and it makes sense the moment you think about cost.
A still image is cheap to make and cheap to throw away. Video costs more. So a
brand that has not found its winner tests wide and cheap on images; a brand
that has found it commits to video, because video is what actually sells and
you only spend that money on something you are confident in.

**What this means for you:** images for finding out, video for scaling. Both
of those are jobs a machine can do at volume — and knowing which stage you are
in tells you which one to point it at.

---

## 4. What the funnel shape suggests

Two clearly different modes are visible here, and they are worth naming
because you will be in one of them.

**Searching.** The acne brand. 162 pitches, mostly images, five versions each,
nothing dominating. This is a brand throwing a lot at the wall, cheaply, to
find out what sticks. It is also the brand running the widest range of *shapes*
in this pack — the misdiagnosis pitch, the failed-products pitch, the scarcity
pitch, the personal confession, the insider — all at once. That is what
searching looks like.

**Scaling.** The other three. Few pitches, heavy video, dozens of versions of
the same idea, most of the account sitting on one or two things. This is a
brand that has stopped asking the question and started pressing on the answer.

There is also a visible difference in what they use to close. Across all 2,626
ads, pitches whose *headline* leads with a discount account for about **11
ads** — under half a percent. Scarcity and stock warnings account for about
**113 ads** — around 4%. The overwhelming majority of the money is on ads that
explain something: why your problem is not what you thought, why the things
you tried failed, what the product is actually made of.

Discounts and deadlines show up mostly as the *last line* of a longer pitch,
not as the pitch itself. That is a real finding and it is the opposite of what
most beginners do first.

---

## 5. What these numbers cannot tell you

Be straight about the edges of this. The sweep can see what is running. It
cannot see:

- **Actual money.** No dollar figure, no daily budget, no cost per sale. Ad
  count is a proxy and nothing more.
- **Whether it is profitable.** A brand can run 313 versions of something and
  still be losing money. Concentration tells you they *believe* it works. It
  does not prove it does.
- **How long anything has been running.** A pitch with 300 versions might have
  been built up over a year or launched last week. There are no dates here, so
  there is no way to tell a proven winner from a big new bet.
- **What happens after the click.** No landing pages, no prices, no checkout,
  no upsells. Everything above is about the ad only. Two brands with identical
  ads can have completely different businesses behind them.
- **Who the ads were shown to.** No audiences, no ages, no locations. Where a
  market is described in this pack, it was read off the copy itself.
- **What they already switched off.** Only live ads were counted. Everything
  that failed and was turned off is invisible — which means you are seeing
  survivors, and survivors always look smarter than the full record would.
- **One brand's market was never labelled.** The supplement brand had no market
  written down; what it sells was read from its own copy. Treat its market
  description as an inference, not a fact.

The honest summary: this data is very good at telling you **what shape a
winning pitch takes**, and it cannot tell you **how much anyone made**. This
pack is built on the first thing only.
